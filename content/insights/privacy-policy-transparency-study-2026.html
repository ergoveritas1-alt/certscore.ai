Your data can outlast your account by years

Privacy-policy wording from 5,000 website reports shows how long companies say they keep information, what you can ask them to delete, and where your data may go.

CertScore.ai · Published 16 October 2026 · Study snapshot from 2 September to 12 October 2026

Close your account, and some of your information may remain on file for years. One privacy policy captured for a new CertScore.ai study says certain contract and transaction records are kept for ten years after a person’s last interaction.

The policy gives business and legal requirements as the reason. Read closely, the sentence is narrower than it first sounds. The clock runs from the last interaction, and the ten-year period applies to particular records. It is not a promise to delete everything when the account closes.

That passage was among 10,485 distinct policy excerpts identified in a study of 5,000 archived website reports. Across the 728 retention passages, 611 (84%) described conditions for keeping information without an explicit numeric period found in the captured wording.

The study found source-linked passages about data retention in 728 reports, privacy rights in 991, and international transfers in 1,305. Another 1,913 contained passages explaining purposes for using personal information. Those figures count the evidence available for inspection. They do not tell us how many other policies contain the same disclosures.

How long policies say information is kept

The ten-year statement ties the period to particular records and a starting point:

Additionally, there is some personal information we need to retain even after you close your account to comply with business and legal requirements, such as personal information related to our contract and business transactions with you, which we retain for ten years after your last interaction with us.

Most of the retention passages took a different approach. In 611 of the 728 captured passages (84%), the analysis found conditions for keeping information but no explicit numeric period. For a reader, that shifts the question from “How many years?” to “What has to happen before this information is no longer needed?”

These periods measure different things. Some concern storage, others deletion, anonymization or a move into an archive. A short period for logs says little about how long billing records might remain. A deadline for anonymization is different from a deadline for deletion.

What a policy gives you to work with

A policy can also tell someone where to start when they want a copy of their information or want it deleted. One passage in the study’s rights examples points to account settings and offers a contact route if those controls are unavailable:

The right to access, update or delete the information We have on You. Whenever made possible, you can access, update or request deletion of Your Personal Data directly within Your account settings section. If you are unable to perform these actions yourself, please contact Us to assist You. This also enables You to receive a copy of the Personal Data We hold about You.

The passage appears in a section about GDPR rights. Its “Whenever made possible” qualification matters: the account-settings route may not always be available. The study did not test either route, but the wording gives a reader a practical place to begin.

Other passages help readers work out what to ask a company. In one example, a policy names a safeguard for transfers to processors outside the European Economic Area:

If we transfer your personal data to third party processors outside of the EEA, we take legally required steps to ensure that appropriate safeguards, such as agreement to Standard Contractual Clauses are in place to protect your personal data.

That gives the reader something specific to follow up on: which transfers the statement covers and where the stated safeguards can be inspected. The conditional “If we transfer” does not establish that a transfer happened.

A fourth example describes possible uses of personal information:

We may use your personal information to administer surveys and other market research and for other research and development purposes, including to analyze and improve the Services and our business.

Here, someone can see that the stated purposes include research and service improvement, then ask which information is used for each. Each passage shows what a policy says, not what the company does.

Regulators are reading too

In March 2026, the European Data Protection Board announced that 25 European data protection authorities would examine transparency and information obligations through its 2026 coordinated enforcement action.

In the United States, California’s CCPA requires covered businesses’ privacy policies to explain consumer rights and how to exercise them. A 2022 California Attorney General settlement required a retailer to correct its policy to disclose that it sold personal information, alongside changes to its opt-out mechanisms.

In its 2024 guidance, the New York Attorney General reported finding 13 high-traffic websites whose privacy controls did not work as described. All the companies resolved the issues after being contacted. At the federal level, the FTC takes enforcement action over companies’ promises to safeguard personal information.

Those examples explain why accurate wording and working controls matter. They do not establish violations in this study, and the same legal duties do not apply to every website.

If you run a website, the same reading works in reverse: does your policy explain which records you keep and why, and does the deletion route it describes actually work?

About the study

CertScore.ai analyzed 5,000 archived reports completed between 2 September and 12 October 2026, selecting the latest eligible report per registrable website domain, then the 5,000 most recent domain representatives. Eligible reports had a completed or completed-limited scan, an identified privacy-policy page, all 11 transparency results, retained wording and recorded source details. Sites can share policies, so this is not a count of 5,000 unique policies. Each counted passage had to be linked to its source page and pass topic-specific text checks. “Distinct” means exact excerpt text, preserving capitalization and punctuation, after trimming surrounding whitespace. It is not a representative sample of the web and does not establish disclosure rates or legal compliance. Non-English text is likely under-counted; retention categories cover English wording only. A period not found in a retained passage may appear elsewhere in the full policy.

All four examples came from policy captures dated 12 October 2026. Website names are omitted, but the exact quotations remain searchable and may identify their sources. Those selected reports retrieved their policy evidence on that date. The study reused archived evidence rather than visiting the sites again. CertScore.ai conducted the research using its own product data and has a commercial interest in the product.

Read selection rules, counting definitions and retention validation

This supplement describes the frozen evidence behind the article Your data can outlast your account by years. It measures captured policy wording in an archive cohort, rather than disclosure prevalence or legal compliance.

Selecting the reports

The archive inventory froze on 12 October 2026 at 19:23:12 UTC with 40,297 unique report files. Eligibility yielded 15,561 reports covering 8,217 registrable report domains. The analysis chose the latest eligible report per domain and then the 5,000 most recent domain representatives, breaking time ties by scan identifier. Selected reports completed between 2 September and 12 October 2026.

Eligible exports had a valid completion time at or before the cutoff; completed or completed-limited status; an explicit privacy-policy surface; all 11 canonical transparency rows without conflicting aliases; at least one content excerpt; at least one content row with a source URL, retrieval timestamp and recorded controller or first-party-brand relationship; and a report projection hash. Excerpts and source details could occur on different rows at this selection stage. The counted positive evidence required them together. Internal product and reserved test, example and localhost domains were excluded. No positive result for a particular content topic was required.

The domain unit uses public-suffix-aware normalization with private suffixes enabled. It is not a verified census of companies or destination domains. Reports can share policies: grouping by each report’s primary attributed policy-source URL produced 4,842 URL groups. These are URL groups, not verified unique policy documents.

Retention coding and its limits

The 728 retention rows contain 728 passage occurrences and 676 exact unique texts. English-language rules identify number words or digits with days, months or years in retention-related sentences, with heading-bound schedule review, then classify numeric periods, criteria without an extracted numeric period, indefinite wording, or unresolved wording. Review-cadence intervals are excluded, and permanent deletion does not mean indefinite retention. Duplicate passages across reports remain in the occurrence counts. A separate search of all 728 excerpts found no retention periods expressed in weeks, hours, minutes or seconds; the only time-unit-like matches were two postal-address references to “Second Floor.”

What the study can support

The cohort is a recency-selected, non-probability snapshot. It is not representative of the web, and repeated policies and multiple topics are not independent observations. No trend, compliance ranking, confidence interval or population estimate is inferred. Non-English text is likely under-counted across topics; retention coding covers English wording only. The checks are not an exhaustive statement of US or EU law.

The analysis preserved historical report labels and reused exports from projection versions v24, v26 and v27. It did not recrawl websites or establish that the wording remains current. Selected export bytes were checked against the archive’s recorded checksums before extraction. Export checksums bind report bytes; they do not authenticate original policy-document bytes.

The four featured examples were selected to illustrate retention, rights, transfers and purposes, rather than as a random sample or endorsements. Their exact quotations, punctuation and capitalization match the retained excerpts. Their recorded policy-retrieval times are on 12 October 2026 UTC. Names are omitted; searchable quotations may still identify their sources. The source mapping and row-level evidence remain private.

CertScore.ai conducted this research using its own product data and has a commercial interest in the product. The regulatory sources provide context rather than findings about the sampled websites.
